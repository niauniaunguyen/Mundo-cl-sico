La religió romana era politeista i oberta: Roma havia anat adoptant déus dels pobles vençuts i dels grecs, i en una ciutat d'un milió d'habitants vinguts de tot el Mediterrani hi convivien molts cultes. Els historiadors van anomenar «cultes orientals» els que van arribar d'Àsia Menor, Egipte, Síria o Pèrsia; avui l'etiqueta es discuteix, perquè cadascun va tenir la seva pròpia història i molts es van tornar molt romans.
Roman religion was polytheistic and open: Rome had gradually adopted gods from conquered peoples and from the Greeks, and in a city of a million inhabitants from all over the Mediterranean many cults lived side by side. Historians called "oriental cults" those that came from Asia Minor, Egypt, Syria or Persia; today the label is debated, because each had its own history and many became thoroughly Roman.

<b>La Gran Mare.</b> Va ser el primer i el més oficial. El 204 aC, en plena guerra contra Anníbal, els llibres sibil·lins van aconsellar portar de Pessinunt, a Frígia, la pedra negra de la Mare de l'Ida. El seu temple del Palatí es va dedicar el 191 aC i els seus jocs, les Megalèsies, se celebraven cada abril amb obres de teatre. Però els seus sacerdots, els gals, eunucs frigis que ballaven amb tambors, escandalitzaven: als ciutadans se'ls va prohibir fer-se gals.
<b>The Great Mother.</b> She was the first and the most official. In 204 BC, in the middle of the war against Hannibal, the Sibylline Books advised bringing the black stone of the Idaean Mother from Pessinus in Phrygia. Her temple on the Palatine was dedicated in 191 BC and her games, the Megalesia, were held every April with plays. But her priests, the galli, Phrygian eunuchs who danced with drums, scandalised people: citizens were forbidden to become galli.

<b>Isis i Serapis.</b> La deessa egípcia va arribar amb els comerciants de Delos i de la Campània. A Roma va ser perseguida durant un segle: el Senat va enderrocar els seus altars, August va prohibir els seus ritus al centre de la ciutat i Tiberi va destruir el seu temple el 19 dC. <b>Isis and Serapis.</b> The Egyptian goddess came with traders from Delos and Campania. In Rome she was persecuted for a century: the Senate pulled down her altars, Augustus banned her rites in the city centre and Tiberius destroyed her temple in AD 19. But her devotees kept growing, and around 40 the great Iseum of the Campus Martius was built, with obelisks and sphinxes brought from Egypt. The Flavians protected it: Vespasian and Titus spent the night there before their triumph of 71. The fire of 80 damaged it and Domitian will rebuild it.

@5f68a2d1
<b>Jueus i cristians.</b> Els jueus de Roma, potser unes quantes desenes de milers, vivien sobretot al Transtíber, parlaven grec i tenien diverses sinagogues. El seu Déu únic i sense imatges, el dissabte i la circumcisió cridaven l'atenció; alguns romans s'hi acostaven i d'altres se'n burlaven. Des del 70 pagaven un impost per a Júpiter Capitolí. De les seves sinagogues havien sortit els primers cristians, un grup encara petit que es reunia en cases i que Neró havia perseguit després de l'incendi del 64.
<b>Jews and Christians.</b> The Jews of Rome, perhaps several tens of thousands, lived mostly across the Tiber, spoke Greek and had several synagogues. Their single God without images, the Sabbath and circumcision attracted attention; some Romans drew close to them and others mocked them. From 70 they paid a tax for Jupiter Capitolinus. From their synagogues had come the first Christians, a still small group that met in houses and that Nero had persecuted after the fire of 64.

The Persian god Mithras, on the other hand, has barely arrived: the first Latin author to mention him, Statius, writes around 90, and the mithraea of Rome date from the 2nd and 3rd centuries. Aquests cultes atreien perquè oferien una cosa que la religió cívica donava menys: una relació personal amb la divinitat, ritus d'iniciació, comunitats de fidels i, de vegades, l'esperança d'una vida feliç després de la mort. Llevat de jueus i cristians, ningú no demanava exclusivitat: es podia ser devot d'Isis, sacrificar a Júpiter i honorar els Lars de casa.
These cults attracted people because they offered something civic religion gave less of: a personal relationship with the divinity, initiation rites, communities of believers and, sometimes, the hope of a happy life after death. Apart from Jews and Christians, nobody demanded exclusivity: one could be devoted to Isis, sacrifice to Jupiter and honour the household Lares.

Isis, la gran deessa egípcia, esposa d'Osiris i mare d'Horus, es va convertir en època hel·lenística en una deessa universal, protectora dels mariners, de les mares i dels qui pateixen. El seu culte va arribar a Itàlia amb els comerciants i a Roma va tenir devots des del segle I aC, malgrat les prohibicions: el Senat va enderrocar diverses vegades els seus altars, August va prohibir els seus ritus dins del pomeri i Tiberi va destruir el seu temple el 19 dC. Isis, the great Egyptian goddess, wife of Osiris and mother of Horus, became in the Hellenistic age a universal goddess, protector of sailors, mothers and those who suffer. Her cult reached Italy with traders, and in Rome she had devotees from the 1st century BC despite the bans: the Senate pulled down her altars several times, Augustus banned her rites inside the pomerium and Tiberius destroyed her temple in AD 19. Under Caligula the great Iseum of the Campus Martius was built, and the Flavians favoured it: Vespasian and Titus spent the night there before their triumph of 71. Her priests had shaven heads and linen garments, and played the sistrum. The main festivals were the Ship of Isis in March and the Isia at the end of October.

Els jueus de Roma eren una comunitat gran i antiga, sobretot al Transtíber: molts descendien de presoners portats per Pompeu el 63 aC i després alliberats. Parlaven sobretot grec i s'organitzaven en diverses sinagogues, on es llegia la Llei cada dissabte. Cèsar i August els van reconèixer el dret a reunir-se i a enviar diners al Temple de Jerusalem; Tiberi i Claudi els van expulsar per un temps. Després de la destrucció del Temple el 70, Vespasià va obligar tots els jueus de l'Imperi a pagar dues dracmes l'any per al temple de Júpiter Capitolí, i milers de presoners de la guerra van ser venuts com a esclaus. The Jews of Rome were a large and old community, mostly across the Tiber: many descended from prisoners brought by Pompey in 63 BC and later freed. They spoke mainly Greek and were organised in several synagogues, where the Law was read every Sabbath. Caesar and Augustus recognised their right to assemble and to send money to the Temple in Jerusalem; Tiberius and Claudius expelled them for a time. After the destruction of the Temple in 70, Vespasian forced all the Jews of the Empire to pay two drachmas a year for the temple of Jupiter Capitolinus, and thousands of war prisoners were sold as slaves. Some Romans, called "God-fearers", attended the synagogues without converting.

El santuari de déus egipcis més gran de Roma, aixecat probablement en temps de Calígula. Un pilò a l'egípcia dona pas al temple d'Isis; al davant s'obre un llarg pati amb obeliscs de granit vermell portats d'Egipte, esfinxs i estàtues de déus amb cap d'animal. The largest sanctuary of Egyptian gods in Rome, probably built in the time of Caligula. An Egyptian-style pylon leads to the temple of Isis; in front opens a long courtyard with red granite obelisks brought from Egypt, sphinxes and statues of animal-headed gods. The priests, with shaven heads and linen garments, open the temple at dawn with water from the Nile, kept in a sacred jar. Vespasian and Titus spent the night here before their triumph of 71. This year's fire has damaged it; Domitian will rebuild it.

The Great Mother of the gods, the Cybele of Phrygia, came to Rome in 204 BC, in the middle of the war against Hannibal, because the Sibylline Books so advised: her image was a black stone brought from Pessinus. This temple was dedicated in 191 BC, next to the hut of Romulus, and Augustus rebuilt it after a fire. In April, during the Megalesia, comedies are performed in front of it, with the audience seated on the steps. Her priests, the galli, are Phrygian eunuchs who dance to the sound of drums and cymbals; Roman citizens are not allowed to become one.

A house of prayer (proseucha) of the Jews of Rome, who have lived mostly in this district since Pompey brought prisoners from Judaea in 63 BC. Every Sabbath they gather here to read the Law and the Prophets in Greek; during the week it is a school, a court and a common house. The inscriptions of the catacombs name more than ten synagogues in Rome: that of the Augustesians, that of the Agrippesians, that of the Hebrews… None from this period has been found in the city: this one is modelled on the 1st-century synagogue at Ostia, with its porch and its basin for washing hands. Above the door, the menorah, the seven-branched lampstand.
