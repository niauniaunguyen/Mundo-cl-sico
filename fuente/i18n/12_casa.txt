La casa atenesa (oikía) s'organitzava al voltant d'un pati obert, amb l'altar de Zeus Herkeios i sovint un pou o una cisterna. Els murs eren de tova sobre un sòcol de pedra, amb poques finestres al carrer. L'andró, amb la porta descentrada i la vora del terra preparada per als divans, era la sala on el senyor rebia els convidats; les dones tenien les seves habitacions (gineceu), de vegades al pis de dalt. L'oikos era també la família i els seus béns, esclaus inclosos. Els sofistes donaven sovint les seves lliçons a cases de rics, com la de Cal·lias que descriu Plató al Protàgores.
The Athenian house (oikía) was arranged around an open courtyard, with the altar of Zeus Herkeios and often a well or a cistern. The walls were mud brick on a stone footing, with few windows onto the street. The andron, with its off-centre door and the floor edge prepared for couches, was the room where the master received his guests; the women had their rooms (gynaikeion), sometimes upstairs. The oikos was also the family and its property, slaves included. Sophists often gave their lessons in rich men's houses, like that of Callias described by Plato in the Protagoras.

Pròdic de Ceos (c. 465-després del 399) va ser sofista i ambaixador de la seva illa a Atenes. Es va fer famós per la «correcció dels noms»: distingir amb precisió paraules que semblen sinònimes, com discutir i barallar-se, o alegrar-se i gaudir. Cobrava una dracma per una conferència breu i cinquanta per la completa. La seva faula d'Hèracles a la cruïlla, entre el Vici i la Virtut, la coneixem per Xenofont (Memorables II 1). Sòcrates l'apreciava i li enviava alumnes.
Prodicus of Ceos (c. 465-after 399) was a sophist and his island's ambassador to Athens. He became famous for the «correctness of names»: precisely distinguishing words that seem synonymous, like disputing and quarrelling, or rejoicing and enjoying. He charged one drachma for a short lecture and fifty for the full one. His fable of Heracles at the crossroads, between Vice and Virtue, is known to us through Xenophon (Memorabilia II 1). Socrates thought well of him and sent him pupils.

@6725ff7d
Per pensar amb claredat. Si a l'Assemblea un diu «valor» i un altre entén «temeritat», discuteixen sense saber de què. Si un jutge confon «voler» amb «desitjar», condemna malament. Les paraules són com les monedes: si no saps quina és de plata i quina de coure, t'enganyen. Abans de discutir, cal posar-se d'acord en els noms.
To think clearly. If in the Assembly one man says «courage» and another understands «rashness», they argue without knowing about what. If a judge confuses «wanting» with «desiring», he condemns wrongly. Words are like coins: if you don't know which is silver and which is copper, you get cheated. Before arguing, you must agree on the names.

Heracles chose the hard road of Virtue. Prodicus asks the pupils what they would have chosen, and each must justify it with a short speech. That was how teaching worked: with a well-built model (an epideixis, or display), which the pupils then imitated and discussed. Xenophon puts this story in Socrates' mouth; for centuries it will be repeated in schools and in paintings.

Hípias d'Elis (c. 460-després del 399), sofista i ambaixador de la seva ciutat, presumia de dominar totes les arts: astronomia, geometria, aritmètica, música, gramàtica, genealogies d'herois i fundacions de ciutats. Hippias of Elis (c. 460-after 399), sophist and ambassador of his city, boasted of mastering every art: astronomy, geometry, arithmetic, music, grammar, genealogies of heroes and foundations of cities. He had a prodigious memory and taught techniques for remembering. It was said that at Olympia he appeared wearing nothing that he had not made himself. He compiled the list of Olympic victors. Plato mocks him in the Greater Hippias and the Lesser Hippias, and in the Protagoras attributes to him the opposition between law, «tyrant of men», and nature.

What is a sophist?

@38e26c50
Sofista ve de sophós, savi. Al principi, sofista era qualsevol savi o poeta. Protàgores va ser el primer a anomenar-se així i a cobrar per ensenyar. Ara molta gent ho fa servir com a insult, per culpa de Sòcrates i els seus amics: diuen que venem la saviesa com els mercaders venen menjar, sense saber si és bo. Nosaltres diem que la feina es paga.
Sophist comes from sophos, wise. At first, a sophist was any wise man or poet. Protagoras was the first to call himself that and charge for teaching. Now many people use it as an insult, thanks to Socrates and his friends: they say we sell wisdom the way merchants sell food, without knowing whether it's good. We say work gets paid.

Protagoras charged a hundred minas, but let a pupil who wasn't satisfied swear in a temple what he thought the lessons were worth, and pay that.

Què és la paradoxa d'Aquil·les i la tortuga?
What is the paradox of Achilles and the tortoise?

@55556dc8
Una de Zenó d'Elea, el deixeble de Parmènides. Aquil·les, el més ràpid, corre darrere d'una tortuga que surt amb avantatge. Quan arriba on era la tortuga, ella ja ha avançat una mica; quan arriba a aquest nou punt, ella ha avançat una altra mica, i així sempre: no l'atrapa mai. Tots sabem que l'atrapa, però on falla el raonament? Amb aquestes paradoxes els nois aprenen a desconfiar dels arguments massa bonics.
One of Zeno of Elea's, Parmenides' pupil. Achilles, the fastest, runs after a tortoise that has a head start. When he reaches where the tortoise was, it has already moved on a little; when he reaches that new point, it has moved on a little more, and so on forever: he never catches it. We all know he does, but where does the reasoning fail? With paradoxes like these the boys learn to distrust arguments that are too pretty.
